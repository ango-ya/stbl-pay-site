What Is a High-Risk Merchant Account? A Plain-English Guide

A high-risk merchant account is a card-payment account for a business that its card processor or acquiring bank treats as higher risk than usual. Stripe, for example, says it must follow rules set by card networks and banking partners, so it cannot support some businesses and gives others extra review[1]. This guide explains why a business gets the label, what it can involve, why "instant approval" claims deserve care, and what non-card options exist, including a yen stablecoin for selling to buyers in Japan.

Updated: November 9, 2026　By Ango-ya LLC

What is a high-risk merchant account?

"High-risk merchant account" is an industry phrase, not a legal term. It usually means a card-payment account at a processor or acquirer that sees the business as carrying more risk than average. Card acceptance runs through card networks such as Visa and Mastercard, and a merchant must keep disputes (also called chargebacks) and fraud at acceptable levels as part of its obligations to those networks[2]. Processors set their own rules on top of the networks' rules, so one processor may decline a business that another accepts. This guide uses public documentation from Stripe and Visa as examples, and other processors may differ.

Why are some businesses classified as high risk?

Public documents point to a few kinds of reasons.

  • The industry or product. Stripe keeps lists of prohibited businesses and of restricted businesses. Restricted ones need additional due diligence, and Stripe says that because of card network rules, requirements of financial partners and its own compliance and legal obligations, it might not be able to approve them[1]. Examples on its restricted list include cryptocurrency exchanges and wallets, money transmitters, online dating services and CBD products[1].
  • The business model. For restricted categories, Stripe says it asks for extra information, such as proof of relevant licenses or more details about the business model[1].
  • Dispute and fraud levels. If disputes or fraud exceed thresholds set by a network such as Visa or Mastercard, the network places the business in one of its monitoring programs[2]. Visa describes its Acquirer Monitoring Program (VAMP) as one that identifies high-fraud parties[3].
  • History. Visa and Mastercard operate databases of terminated merchants, Mastercard's MATCH and Visa's VMSS. Per Stripe, acquirers must screen applicants against them before approval[4].

Where a business falls is up to its processor. If you are unsure, ask the processor which of its policies applies to you.

What does a high-risk merchant account involve?

The public sources describe these things. Which of them applies to you depends on the processor and your agreement.

What public documentation says about higher-risk card accounts
Item What the sources say
Extra review Restricted businesses need additional due diligence. Approval is specific to each service, and Stripe says it may modify or revoke it at any time[1]
Reserves In Stripe's terms, a Reserve is collateral funds that Stripe holds and controls to satisfy liabilities or potential liabilities the user incurs[5]
Monitoring programs Above a network's thresholds, the network places the business in a monitoring program, and monthly fines and additional fees can apply until levels fall in a sustained way[2]
Loss of card acceptance Stripe says that failing to meet a program's requirements within the specified period can result in the network refusing to process further payments, which puts the ability to accept card payments at risk[2]
Termination lists Acquirers report merchants they terminate for fraud, severe policy violations or excessive chargebacks. A listing is a severe industry-wide restriction, as most processors automatically reject listed businesses or principal owners. It stays active for 5 years, and only the acquirer that listed the business can request removal[4]

Pricing and reserve terms differ by processor and are set in each agreement, so compare the written terms before you sign. We did not find a primary source that gives typical figures, so this guide does not quote any.

Is there "instant approval" for high-risk merchant accounts?

Card acceptance is approved by an acquirer or processor, not by a website that markets accounts, and each one runs its own underwriting. Stripe's documentation shows why a firm promise is hard to give. Acquirers must screen every applicant against the terminated merchant databases before approval[4], restricted businesses may be asked for licenses or business-model details[1], and even an approval can be modified or revoked later[1]. Be wary of any offer that promises sure or instant approval before the processor has reviewed your business. Read the agreement for reserve, fee and termination terms, and ask who the acquiring bank is.

What are the alternatives to card payments?

If cards are not available or not a good fit, the options depend on where your buyers are. Ask a processor which payment methods it supports for your business. Some methods work differently from cards. For example, Stripe says its Konbini method used in Japan does not support the standard dispute process, so customers cannot file chargebacks[6]. For other methods, see our guides on payment methods in Japan, konbini payment and accepting stablecoin payments. Each option has its own terms, limits and eligibility rules, so check them before you rely on one.

Frequently asked questions

Is a high-risk merchant account the same as being on the MATCH list?

No. A high-risk label is a processor's own risk assessment. MATCH is Mastercard's database of businesses that acquirers terminated for severe rule violations, fraud or high chargebacks. Stripe says most processors automatically reject listed businesses, and listings stay active for 5 years.

Can I get a high-risk merchant account with instant approval?

Card acceptance is approved by the acquirer or processor, and each one sets its own checks. Stripe says it asks for extra information such as licenses or business-model details for restricted businesses, and that approval can be modified or revoked. Be wary of any promise of sure or instant approval.

What is a reserve on a merchant account?

In Stripe's terms, a Reserve is collateral funds that Stripe holds and controls to cover liabilities or potential liabilities the user incurs. Other processors have their own terms, so read the agreement of the one you use.

What happens if my chargebacks get too high?

Stripe says that if disputes or fraud exceed a card network's thresholds, the network places the business in a monitoring program. You can incur monthly fines and additional fees until levels fall in a sustained way.

Do card network rules apply to a stablecoin payment?

Card network rules apply to payments made on card networks. STBLpay does not use card networks, so card-network merchant underwriting and its high-risk categories do not apply to it. Its own terms of service and legal requirements still apply.

Is STBLpay a replacement for a US card account?

No. Buyers pay in yen from Japan and need a JPYC EX account, which is for individuals residing in Japan. STBLpay is for selling to buyers in Japan. It is under development, and the launch date has not been set (as of November 2026).
